# Dehydration

## Overview

Dehydration occurs when the body loses more fluid than it takes in and no longer has enough fluid to function normally. It can range from mild to severe. Fluids and electrolytes, which are minerals involved in fluid balance and other body functions, may both need to be replaced.

## Causes and Risk Factors

Possible causes include diarrhea, vomiting, heavy sweating, fever, excessive urination, and not drinking enough fluids. Infants and young children, older adults, people with certain chronic illnesses, and people exercising or working in hot conditions can be more vulnerable. Some medicines and illnesses increase fluid losses.

## Symptoms and Warning Signs

In adults, signs may include thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, reduced urination, tiredness, and dizziness. In babies and young children, warning signs include a dry mouth, few or no tears, fewer wet diapers, unusual sleepiness, irritability, or sunken-looking eyes. Confusion, fainting, very little or no urination, rapid breathing, or a fast heartbeat may indicate severe dehydration and require urgent medical care.

## Diagnosis

A health care professional can assess dehydration by asking about symptoms and fluid losses, examining the patient, and checking vital signs. Blood tests may be used to assess electrolytes such as sodium and potassium and kidney function. Urine tests may help evaluate dehydration and possible causes.

## Treatment and Prevention

Mild dehydration can often be addressed by drinking fluids; oral rehydration solutions can help replace fluids and electrolytes, particularly in children with diarrhea or vomiting. Severe dehydration may require intravenous fluids in a hospital. Adequate fluid intake is especially important during illness, exercise, and hot weather; the amount needed varies by person and situation.
